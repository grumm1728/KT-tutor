# Component-grain ideas and the representational development of distance

Research note for issue #14. Written 2026-07-31.

Two questions:

1. **Priority.** Is the representational progression for *distance-as-interval* (counting order →
   number path → ten frame → number line) documented? Is the fencepost error a *stage signature* or
   a momentary slip?
2. **Secondary.** Does any published framework enumerate *component ideas* — a grain below strategy?

---

## Bottom line up front

**Priority question: the project's hypothesis is substantially correct and better supported than
expected — but the support comes from the *measurement* literature, not the arithmetic literature.**

1. **The number path / number line distinction is established and load-bearing**, with a canonical
   citation (NRC 2009, whose committee included Fuson, Clements and Sarama). The rationale given is
   exactly the project's: young children cannot see the units on a number line, so they attend to
   the numbers rather than the intervals.
2. **The fencepost error is documented as a stage signature, not a slip — under a different name.**
   The literature calls it the **"hatch mark counting" error** (Kwon, Congdon, Ping & Levine 2024),
   or counting hash marks rather than intervals. It is a *systematic error strategy* that persists
   from early elementary into middle school and is stable absent targeted instruction. **This is the
   single most useful finding in this note.**
3. **The discrete-vs-continuous step has been experimentally isolated, and the result is dramatic.**
   Solomon, Vasilyeva, Huttenlocher & Levine (2015) crossed *continuous ruler vs. row of discrete
   units* with *aligned vs. shifted*. Shifted-with-discrete-units: good performance. Shifted-with-
   ruler: **at chance.** That is the number path / number line contrast, and it is the largest
   single effect in this note.
4. **The ten frame is the weak link.** It is a well-supported tool, but I found no source placing it
   as a representational stage *for distance* between discrete counting and the number line. It is
   consistently framed as a tool for *composing/decomposing to a landmark*, which is a different
   component. Step 3 of the progression is the project's own synthesis. Say so.

**Secondary question: the answer is a qualified negative, with one strong exception.**

5. **No framework enumerates component ideas at this grain for early arithmetic.** CGI, Parrish,
   Clements & Sarama, the CCSS Progressions and McIntosh/Reys/Reys all sit at the strategy level or
   the level-of-conception level. Nobody has published the component list the project wants.
6. **But the *layer* has been named and operationalized — in physics.** Minstrell's **facets** and
   **facet clusters** are precisely "individual pieces of knowledge that learners use to construct
   understanding in a given situation," grouped into clusters and **ranked by how problematic they
   are**. That is the project's component-with-polarity, built forty years ago, with a working
   diagnostic system (DIAGNOSER) on top of it. The project is not inventing a new grain of analysis;
   it is **populating an existing grain for a domain nobody populated it for.** That is a much
   stronger claim to make than "we invented components," and it connects directly to the
   knowledge-in-pieces citation already in CLAUDE.md.

## 1. Number path vs. number line — **established, with a named rationale**

**Verdict: verified.** The distinction is standard in early-years guidance and traceable past blog
posts to a high-trust source.

- A **number path** is a *count model*. Numbers are rectangles/cells; each can be counted. The unit
  is a thing you can see and point at.
- A **number line** is a *length model*. Each number is its distance from zero. Numbers are
  dimensionless points; the unit is the **space between** marks.

**The rationale — this is the fencepost mechanism, stated in advance:**

> young children have difficulties with the number line representation because they have difficulty
> seeing the units — they need to see things, so they focus on the numbers instead of on the
> lengths. They may count the starting point 0 and then be off by one, or they focus on the spaces
> and are confused by the location of the numbers at the ends of the spaces.

Attributed to NRC (2009), *Mathematics Learning in Early Childhood: Paths Toward Excellence and
Equity* (Committee on Early Childhood Mathematics; Cross, Woods & Schweingruber, eds.). Fuson,
Clements and Sarama were all on that committee, which is why secondary sources cite this variously
as "NRC 2009" and "Fuson et al. 2009."

⚠️ **Not page-verified.** The passage is reproduced near-identically by Didax, Visual Math Journeys,
and Cambridge Mathematics Espresso #35, which is good evidence it is a real quote, but I did not
confirm it against the report. **Page-verify before quoting it in a talk.** The report is free at
nap.nationalacademies.org.

**Recommendation in the literature:** number paths through roughly grade 1; number line from around
grade 2 (age 7–8).

**What this buys the project.** The bottom two steps of the proposed progression — *counting order*
and *number path* — are not the project's invention, and the claim that they are **different
representations of one idea with the discrete one accessible earlier** is the standard position.

**Caveat, stated plainly.** Most accessible writing on this is practitioner-facing (Didax, Great
Minds/Eureka, teacher blogs). It is a *curricular design principle derived from measurement
research*, not a heavily-studied research finding in its own right. The research weight is in §3.

## 2. Clements & Sarama learning trajectories — **partial fit; do not overstate**

**Verdict: they encode representational level for *length measurement*, which is where
distance-as-interval actually lives in their scheme. There is no Number Line trajectory, and no
trajectory for distance between two numbers.**

The 20 trajectory topics on learningtrajectories.org are: Subitizing; Counting; Comparing and
Ordering Number; Adding / Subtracting; Composing Numbers; Multiplying / Dividing; Fractions;
Patterns, Structure, and Algebraic Thinking; 2D Shapes; Composing 2D Shapes; Disembedding Shapes;
3D Shapes; Composing 3D Shapes; Spatial Visualization and Imagery; Spatial Orientation;
Measurement: Length; Measurement: Area; Measurement: Volume; Measurement: Angle and Turn;
Classification and Data Analysis. **No "Number Line," no "Number Sense."**

### 2a. Measurement: Length — the levels, verbatim and in order

1. Length Senser: Foundations
2. Length Quantity Recognizer
3. Length Direct Comparer
4. Length Indirect Comparer
5. Serial Orderer to 5 (Length)
6. **End-to-End Length Measurer**
7. Serial Orderer to 6+ (Length)
8. **Length Unit Relater and Repeater**
9. **Length Measurer**
10. **Conceptual Ruler Measurer**
11. Integrated Conceptual Path Measurer
12. Abstract Length Measurer

"End-to-End Length Measurer," verbatim:

> Lays units end to end to measure an object or a distance. May not recognize the need for
> equal-length units or be able to measure if there are fewer units that needed. The ability to
> apply resulting measures to comparison situations develops later in this level.

**Read against the project's progression, the shape matches:** laying discrete units end to end
(≈ number path / snap cubes) precedes unit *iteration* and relating unit size to count (Length Unit
Relater and Repeater), which precedes internalizing the ruler as a mental object (Conceptual Ruler
Measurer ≈ number line as measure). So yes — C&S do encode representational level for this idea,
framed as *level of unit conception*, which is arguably the better framing.

**Where the fit breaks, and it matters:** these are levels for measuring *physical objects with
physical units*. The project's component is distance between two *numbers* — 17 to 20 — with no
object present. That sits at the join between the Length trajectory and the Counting /
Comparing-and-Ordering trajectories, and C&S do not stitch that join explicitly. **Using their
level names for a numeric-distance component would be borrowing, not citing.** Don't put "End-to-End
Length Measurer" on a KT-tutor component and imply it is theirs.

### 2b. Adding / Subtracting — the levels, verbatim and in order

1. Arithmetic Senser: Foundations
2. Preverbal +/-
3. Small Number +/-
4. Find Result +/-
5. Make It N
6. Change +/-
7. Counting Strategies +/-
8. **Part-Whole +/-**
9. **Numbers-in-Numbers +/-**
10. **Deriver +/- (Adding/Subtracting)**
11. Problem Solver +/- (Adding/Subtracting)
12. Multidigit +/- (Adding/Subtracting)

Two of these are directly relevant:

- **Deriver +/-** — described as using flexible strategies and derived combinations
  (e.g. "7 + 7 is 14, so 7 + 8 is 15"), thinking of three numbers within a sum simultaneously, and
  including **Break Apart to Make Ten (BAMT)**. **BAMT is Clements & Sarama's name for the
  make-ten / bridging strategy.** This is the closest published label for the spine's strategy.
- **Numbers-in-Numbers +/-** and **Part-Whole +/-** are the levels where an embedded-number /
  part-whole conception is named. 🔬 I could not get their verbatim descriptions off the site; the
  level pages exist but the description blocks did not extract. Worth one more pass.

**Note the grain.** Even here, C&S's units are **levels of a child**, not **components of an
utterance**. "Deriver" describes a child; the project wants to say "this utterance evidences
decomposition-preserving-the-whole, positively, and count-to-distance mapping, negatively." Those
are different objects. See §5.

## 3. Fencepost / off-by-one — **documented as a stage signature. Its name is "hatch mark counting."**

**Verdict: verified. This is the strongest result in the note.**

Kwon, Congdon, Ping & Levine (2024) characterize the error verbatim as:

> the "hatch mark counting" error, involves counting the hatch marks that correspond to the object
> being measured rather than the spatial interval units

**The diagnostic structure is exactly the project's problem.** Hatch mark counting **produces the
correct answer whenever the object is aligned with zero**, and produces an answer **one greater than
correct** when the object is shifted. Under ordinary conditions the misconception is invisible: a
child can look fully competent while reading marks rather than intervals. It surfaces only on a
"shifted" or "broken ruler" item.

**Stage signature, not slip.** The literature treats it as a *systematic error strategy*:

- It persists from early elementary through middle school.
- More than three quarters of third graders answer shifted-ruler items incorrectly.
- Error patterns are stable absent targeted instruction.
- Kwon et al.'s intervention is **disconfirming evidence** — you have to actively break it. That is
  a claim about conceptual structure, not about attention.

Prior-art chain cited by Kwon et al. for this misconception: Carpenter et al. (1988); Lindquist &
Kouba (1989); Kamii & Clark (1997); Lehrer et al. (1998); Solomon et al. (2015). Lehrer (2003) is
the usual reference for the framing that **intervals are the spaces between marks, not the marks
themselves.** ⚠️ None read directly; attribution is via Kwon et al.

### 3a. Solomon et al. (2015) — the experiment that isolates the representation

Abstract, verbatim (ERIC EJ1079407):

> Understanding measurement units is critical to mathematics and science learning, but it is a topic
> that American students find difficult. In 3 studies, we investigated the challenges underlying
> this difficulty in kindergarten and second grade by comparing performance on different versions of
> a linear measurement task. Children measured crayons that were either aligned or shifted relative
> to the left edge of either a continuous ruler or a row of discrete units. The alignment (aligned,
> shifted) and the measuring tool (ruler, discrete units) were crossed to form 4 types of problems.
> Study 1 showed good performance in both grades on both types of aligned problems as well as on the
> shifted problems with discrete units. In contrast, performance was at chance on the shifted ruler
> problems. Study 2 showed that performance on shifted discrete unit problems declined when numbers
> were placed on the units, particularly for kindergarteners, suggesting that on the shifted ruler
> problems, the presence of numbers may have contributed to children's difficulty. However, Study 3
> showed that the difficulty on the shifted ruler problems persisted even when the numbers were
> removed from the ruler. Taken together, these findings suggest that there are multiple challenges
> to understanding measurement, but that a key challenge is conceptualizing the ruler as a set of
> countable spatial interval units.

Full citation: Solomon, T. L., Vasilyeva, M., Huttenlocher, J., & Levine, S. C. (2015). Minding the
gap: Children's difficulty conceptualizing spatial intervals as linear measurement units.
*Developmental Psychology, 51*(11), 1564–1573.

**Three things the project should take from this:**

- **Discrete units vs. continuous ruler is a real, large, experimentally isolated variable.** Same
  child, same question, same day: fine on discrete, at chance on the ruler. This is the number path
  / number line step of the progression, measured.
- **Study 2 is a gift.** Putting *numbers on the discrete units* made performance **worse**. Numerals
  actively pull attention away from the intervals. That is the counting-order → number-path boundary
  in the progression, and it says the two lowest steps genuinely conflict rather than nest neatly.
- **Study 3 rules out the easy explanation.** Removing numbers from the ruler did **not** fix it. So
  the difficulty is not "numerals distract" alone — it is conceptualizing the ruler as a set of
  countable spatial interval units. The project's *distance-as-interval* component is, near enough,
  this paper's conclusion sentence.

### 3b. What this means for the demo, concretely

The project's worry — *a stage signature and a slip look identical in one utterance and are told
apart only across turns* — is **the literature's worry too**, and the literature's answer is
directly usable:

- You do not separate them by looking harder at the utterance. You separate them by **changing the
  item so the two hypotheses predict different answers.** The shifted ruler *is* the measurement
  field's card mechanic — an item engineered so that the misconception, normally invisible, produces
  a distinctive wrong answer.
- The teacher move **"show me the 4?"** is doing the same work: it forces the student to externalize
  *which objects were counted*. If they recite number names ("18, 19, 20 — and 17") you are looking
  at a representation-level phenomenon; if they immediately say "oh, 3," you are looking at a slip.
- **This is a citable justification for the card mechanic**, not merely a design intuition. It is
  probably the strongest external validation available for the third leg of the project.

## 4. Ten frame as a distinct representational stage — **not established**

**Verdict: negative. Do not claim the literature supports the ten frame as a stage for *distance*.**

What *is* documented: ten frames support subitizing, five-and-ten structuring, and composing/
decomposing relative to a landmark. Attributed to Van de Walle (1988) and Bobis (1988), with the
common recommendation to use a **five-frame** first and move to the ten-frame after a week or two.
Fuson's "ten-structured conceptions" work (Fuson et al. 1997, *JRME* 28(2), 130–162) is the research
anchor for ten-structuring generally.

But that is a representation of **part–whole structure relative to a landmark** — the project's
*decomposition-preserving-the-whole* and *landmark* components. It is **not** a representation of
distance. The honest reading: seeing "3 empty cells needed to complete the landmark" is a
**complement** reading, not a **distance** reading. They are numerically identical here, and the
project may well be right that the ten frame is where a child first meets the two as the same
quantity — but **no source I found makes that identification as a developmental step.**

**Recommendation:** keep the ten frame in the progression if it is pedagogically useful, but label
it as the project's own synthesis rather than a cited stage. It is also arguably in a different
column from the other three: counting order, number path and number line are all *linear* models of
the interval 17→20; the ten frame is a *containment* model of the complement. That difference is
worth stating rather than smoothing over.

## 5. Does any framework enumerate component ideas below strategy?

### 5a. In early arithmetic — **no**

Checked, and each sits above the grain the project wants:

- **CGI** (Carpenter et al.) — enumerates **strategies** (direct modeling, counting on, derived
  facts) and **problem types**. See `cgi-strategy-taxonomy.md` for the detail; the finding there
  that the strategies are *not* developmentally ordered applies here too.
- **Parrish, *Number Talks*** — enumerates **strategies**: Counting All, Counting On,
  Doubles/Near Doubles, Making Tens, Making Landmark/Friendly Numbers, Compensation, Breaking into
  Place Value, Adding Up in Chunks. Parrish's "big ideas" framing is about what a *problem string*
  is designed to surface, not a component decomposition of an utterance. ⚠️ Book not read.
- **Clements & Sarama** — enumerates **levels of a child** (§2). Right vocabulary for development,
  wrong object for utterance analysis.
- **CCSS Progressions (K–5 CC/OA)** — enumerates **Level 1/2/3 methods** (count all → count on →
  convert to easier problem). **This is the closest genuine hit.** In unpacking "counting on" the
  Progression does name sub-conceptions:
  - the **embedded number** — an addend "perceived simultaneously as an addend and as part of the
    total," so counting can be abbreviated by starting at that addend's number word;
  - **keeping track** — "some method of keeping track (fingers, objects, mentally imaged objects,
    body motions, other count words)."

  Those are genuinely component-grain, and **"keeping track" is arguably the arithmetic-side
  ancestor of the project's count-to-distance-mapping component.** But they appear as *prose
  analysis of one strategy*, not as an enumerated, reusable component vocabulary. There is no list.
  ⚠️ Wording above is from search snippets; the Progressions PDF is image-encoded and I could not
  certify it verbatim. **Verify before quoting.**
- **McIntosh, Reys & Reys (1992)** — a **curriculum/assessment taxonomy** of number sense
  (number knowledge and skills; operation knowledge and skills; applying these in computational
  settings, with sub-strands on meaning and size of numbers, multiple representations, benchmarks,
  and so on). It is a map of a *domain*, not of an *utterance*. ⚠️ Not read — the open-access FLM
  PDF is image-encoded; structure above is from secondary sources and should be treated as
  provisional. **This lead did not pay off and I would not spend more time on it.**

**So: nobody has published the component list this project wants for early arithmetic. The project
is coining.** That is a fine position — but it should be stated, not obscured by hanging borrowed
labels on the components.

### 5b. The layer itself has been named — in physics

**This is the find that changes the framing.** Two overlapping traditions define exactly this grain:

**Minstrell's facets.** A **facet** is a piece of knowledge or reasoning that a learner uses to
construct understanding in a given situation — Minstrell's own gloss is "slight generalizations from
what students actually say or do in the classroom" (Minstrell 2001). Facets for a concept are
grouped into a **cluster**, typically two to six facets, organized around interpreting a situation
or around a conceptual idea. Crucially, within a cluster facets are

> coded loosely numerically according to how problematic they are

on a 0–9 scale, where 0 is the target understanding and higher numbers are more problematic;
"more problematic facets generally require more instructional effort to bring the student to optimal
understanding."

Read that against the project's spec. Component ideas, **derived from what students actually say**,
grouped into clusters around one situation, each carrying a **polarity/productiveness value**,
used to drive a diagnostic that picks the next question. That is the right pane. It was built for
physics in the late 1980s and shipped as **DIAGNOSER** (Hunt & Minstrell 1996; Minstrell 2001;
Thissen-Roe, Hunt & Minstrell in *JIME*), where each assessment item's distractors are written to
correspond to specific problematic facets — i.e. **items engineered so competing components predict
different answers.** Same mechanic as the shifted ruler in §3, same mechanic as the project's cards.

**diSessa's knowledge in pieces / p-prims.** The theoretical parent. P-prims are **sub-conceptual**
knowledge elements — "not concepts, but subconceptual pieces of knowledge" — which are **neither
correct nor incorrect in themselves** but can be appropriately or inappropriately applied, and are
**activated in concert with other elements**. CLAUDE.md already cites Smith, diSessa & Roschelle
(1994) for "conceptions are knowledge in transition, not bugs." Worth noting that the *same* paper's
tradition also supplies the **grain** argument, not just the anti-deficit argument — and that the
"neither correct nor incorrect, activated in context" property is a direct warrant for the project's
insistence that the right pane never renders a component with a red ✗. The framework has since been
extended to mathematics (e.g. "Seeds of algebraic thinking," *ZDM* 2022), but **not, as far as I
found, to early arithmetic strategy talk.**

**Adjacent but a worse fit:** the **KLI framework** (Koedinger, Corbett & Perfetti 2012,
*Cognitive Science* 36, 757–798) defines *knowledge components* and is explicitly grain-size-aware,
and it is the intellectual home of the KC in dialogueKT. But a KC there is a unit of *what is to be
learned*, sized for instructional design and model fitting — closer to the paper's KCs than to the
project's components. Useful for a sentence connecting the project's components to the KT
literature's KCs; not a source for the component list.

**How to say this.** "We are inventing a new unit of analysis" is a weak claim and invites the
objection that it is unfalsifiable. "**The facet-grain has been established and operationalized
since Minstrell (1987/2001); it has never been populated for early arithmetic strategy talk, and
verbal explanation input is what makes populating it possible**" is a much stronger claim, is
defensible, and slots the project into a lineage the CLAUDE.md already half-cites. ⚠️ Minstrell's own
chapter is not read — the definitions above are quoted from a secondary open-access source that
quotes him. Read the chapter before leaning on it hard.

## 6. Naming table for the five components

| Project component | Established name? | Nearest published label | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Selecting a landmark** | **Partially** | "Making Landmark/Friendly Numbers" (Parrish); "benchmark numbers" (McIntosh/Reys/Reys strand); "landmark numbers" (Fosnot & Dolk) | The *word* is established. But all uses name a **strategy** ("make a landmark number"), not the **component** ("this student can select an appropriate landmark"). Reusing the word is safe and legible; asserting a citation for the component is not. |
| **The bridging structure** (one addend splits into two parts playing different roles) | **No — but the strategy has three names** | "Break Apart to Make Ten (BAMT)" (Clements & Sarama, Deriver level); "make a ten" / "decomposing a number leading to a ten" (CCSS 1.OA.6); "bridging through ten" (UK usage) | The **strategy** is well named — use BAMT or make-a-ten and cite it. The **component** — that the split assigns *asymmetric roles* to the two parts (one closes the gap, one is the remainder) — is unnamed. **Coining.** |
| **Decomposition preserving the whole** (4+4=8, so the split is valid) | **Close** | "Part-Whole +/-" and "Numbers-in-Numbers +/-" (Clements & Sarama levels); "embedded number" (CCSS OA Progression); Fuson's "ten-structured conceptions" | The idea is thoroughly established. **"Embedded number" is the best available term** and is worth adopting verbatim — it names precisely the conception that a quantity is simultaneously a part and inside the whole. Note again these are published as *levels of a child*, not components of an utterance. |
| **Distance between two numbers as an interval** | **Yes, in measurement** | "spatial interval unit" / "conceptualizing the ruler as a set of countable spatial interval units" (Solomon et al. 2015); "intervals are the spaces between marks, not the marks themselves" (Lehrer 2003) | **Cite Solomon et al. directly.** The strongest external anchor of the five. Caveat: published for *physical length*, not for distance between two numbers — the transfer is the project's step. |
| **Count-to-distance mapping** (fencepost lives here) | **Yes** | **"hatch mark counting" error** (Kwon et al. 2024); "counting hash marks rather than intervals"; "keeping track" (CCSS OA Progression, for the counting-on side) | **Adopt "hatch mark counting."** It is a real published term for exactly this, with evidence that it is a persistent stage signature rather than a slip, and with a documented diagnostic (the shifted item). Best-supported component in the set. |

**Summary of the naming position.** Two of five components (**distance-as-interval**,
**count-to-distance mapping**) have solid published names and citations, both from the measurement
literature. One (**decomposition preserving the whole**) has a good adoptable term, *embedded
number*. One (**selecting a landmark**) has an established word attached to a different object. One
(**the bridging structure as role-assignment**) the project is coining outright.

That is an honest and defensible distribution. The right move is to **mark each component's
provenance in the artifact itself** — cited / adapted / coined — rather than to present five
uniformly-authoritative labels. Given that the project's validation story is *plausibility and
inspectability rather than accuracy*, showing where a name comes from is the same virtue as showing
which utterance fragment triggered a hypothesis.
